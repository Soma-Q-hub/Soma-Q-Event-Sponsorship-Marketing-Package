The plan on one page

One message, one action, two paths.

The booth does three things in order: stops a person walking past, starts a real conversation, and captures a contact that can be followed up within a day. Everything printed serves one of those jobs, or it is not printed. The event is Saturday, October 24, 2026, seventeen days from the date of this document, so the timeline in the order guide is part of the plan.

GoalsGrow the email list and the contact list (both, with consent), and book free discovery calls from the best conversations.
AudienceAdults only. High-performing leaders, mostly men at this event, plus owners and HR or team leads. You are a regular sponsor, so some visitors already know you.
Budget$500 to $1,500 for printing and swag combined. Spend concentrates on what people see and scan, not on giveaways.

The six rules this package follows

  • One headline, readable in three seconds. A question about stress, with the outcome underneath. No service lists on the banner.
  • Four free first steps, always the same four. Burnout Signal Check, the free ebook, the weekly reset, and a free discovery call. Each has its own QR on the banner, the counter sign and the card, and each is tagged so we can see which one people choose.
  • Scanning must work at standing height. QR codes sit at roughly chest height on the banner, on the counter and in the hand, never on the floor-level table front or the backdrop.
  • Earn the swag. Everyone who talks to us gets a card, which is cheap and useful. A premium keepable goes only to people who give a contact or book a call, so every unit of spend is tied to a lead.
  • Capture before they walk away. The capture tap or scan happens during the conversation, not after, because intent fades the moment they turn the corner.
  • Track every printed piece. Each QR carries its own tag (banner, sign, card, organizations sheet), so after the event we know which piece actually produced scans.

Start from the stranger

Most people walking by have never heard of this.

A man at a summit, between sessions, coffee in hand, has not heard the phrase "nervous system alignment" and has no reason to care about it yet. He does know what a short fuse feels like, what three hours of bad sleep does to a Tuesday, and what it is to be good at the job and tired of it. The booth earns his attention in his words first, and introduces ours only after he is already nodding.

Two minutes (with the posters)Walk the four visuals in order: the loop (what is happening), the shift (what to do about it), the outcomes (what changes), the body map (let them find it in themselves). Each one answers a single question a stranger would ask.

How the dot-sticker poster works

Put the body-map poster on an easel at the front corner of the table, with a sheet of 3/4 inch round stickers beside it. Anyone can place a dot without talking to you. Your opening question to anyone who does is "What do you do next when it shows up?", which leads straight into the loop. This is my recommendation, not something I found evidence for; the earlier evidence table already notes that claims about interactive booths are weakly supported, so count how many people place a dot and how many of those start a conversation, and keep it only if the numbers justify it.

Before the event: buy two colors of stickers (one for "me", one for "someone on my team", which doubles as the individual versus organization signal), a tabletop easel for each of two posters, and foam board or a similar mount for the other two if you will tape them to a wall or the backdrop. I have not found poster printing prices, so ask the printer for a quote on 24 x 36 in prints and mounting.

Language check on the new visuals. The posters use "built to restore" and "designed to build", not promises. They do not say research proves anything, do not name breathwork, and do not use clinical or coaching language. "Pressure runs in a loop" is a plain-language framing taken from your own Pressure Loop graphic; if you want a research-supported statement of the mechanism on a printed piece, tell me and I will check it against the research citations master first.

What the evidence says

Researched, with the confidence level stated.

I searched for evidence behind each major booth decision. Much of what circulates is vendor marketing repeated without a primary source, so each finding is rated by how well it is actually supported. Please verify anything you plan to quote externally.

QuestionWhat I foundSupportHow we use it
Does fast follow-up matter?The most cited speed finding is a 2011 Harvard Business Review study with MIT and InsideSales.com, reported through secondary sources, finding firms that contacted web leads within an hour were nearly seven times as likely to qualify them. It studied online leads, not event leads. Event-specific multipliers (3x, 6x, 9x) appear widely without sources.ModerateFollow up the same day, and no later than 24 hours. Treat the exact multiplier as unproven.
Do most event leads go unfollowed?A figure of roughly 80% never followed up is attributed to CEIR across many blogs, but I could not find the original report, and other sources give 70% or 20%. A 2013 CMO Council and E2MA survey reported only 6% of respondents said they converted tradeshow leads extremely well.WeakInternal caution only. Do not quote the 80% figure publicly. The practical lesson, a written follow-up plan, stands either way.
Do promotional products work?ASI's own studies (an industry trade body, so not independent) report about 85% recall of the advertiser, and that apparel and outerwear are kept longest. Item rankings differ between ASI editions (for example calendars rank last in one and longest in another). Pens and similar items are kept for months, not years.ModerateSkip generic giveaways. Spend on one useful, visible keepable, and only for qualified contacts.
Does the three-second headline rule hold?It is a design heuristic repeated by booth builders, not a measured finding. The advice is consistent: one headline stating the outcome, at eye level, with the primary message above crowd height.HeuristicOne large question headline in the upper third of the banner.
Does interaction raise dwell time?Directionally plausible and widely claimed. Multipliers such as 3 to 5 times and 50% more traffic cite no methodology. Baseline dwell at static booths is reported as roughly 10 to 60 seconds, also unsourced.WeakThe quiz on the visitor's own phone is the interaction. Measure our own dwell and conversion instead of relying on these numbers.
Can POPL and Nimble capture leads?Popl states that it supports NFC and QR sharing, custom lead forms with consent boxes and qualifying questions, offline capture, card scanning, and sync to Salesforce, HubSpot, Zoho, Outlook and Zapier. Nimble documents an AI business card scanner in its mobile app and lists tags, smart lists and email sequences, though I could not confirm tags or sequences can be managed from the phone.Vendor claimsPopl is the primary capture tool. Nimble is the contact system of record. See the capture section for the verification list.
Plain conclusion. No source I found proves a specific booth design wins. The defensible strategy is the one that is cheap to test: put a different tag on each printed piece, capture every conversation the same way, and keep only what produced leads at the next event.

Booth anatomy: where each piece goes

Stand to the side of the table. People approach an open space more readily than a barrier; this is common booth practice rather than a measured finding.

Written for how this audience talks

Design stays neutral so the booth reads as inviting to anyone, and the language does the work of connecting with men: direct sentences, the physical and practical signals (sleep, tension, heavy decisions, a short fuse), and the framing of pressure as something trainable. There is no self-care, glow or journey vocabulary on any piece. If the event data later shows an audience that is overwhelmingly men, a second card with one men-focused headline is a small, low-risk addition, and I would test it against the neutral card rather than replace it.

Setup checklist, do all of it before the event

  • POPL lead form. Fields: name, email, "I am here for: myself / my team / just learning", an optional phone number, and a consent checkbox for email follow-up. Add a separate, unchecked consent for text messages if you intend to text.
  • POPL to Nimble. POPL lists Salesforce, HubSpot, Zoho, Outlook and Zapier as sync options and does not list Nimble by name in the sources I found. Verify whether a Zapier connection to Nimble exists on your plans, and test one lead end to end. If it does not, plan to export the POPL leads to a spreadsheet the same evening and import them into Nimble.
  • Quiz to Nimble. The quiz posts to a Google Form. Decide whether Nimble imports from that sheet, or whether your platform build replaces the quiz capture with a direct lead capture. Until then, plan a manual import on the same evening.
  • Nimble tags and sequences. Create the tags and the five-message follow-up sequence on the desktop site ahead of time. I could not confirm they can be managed from the phone app, so do not plan to build them at the booth.
  • Test on the real devices. Reviewers report NFC tap problems on some older phones, so the counter sign always carries the QR as well as the tap instruction. Bring a charger and a battery pack, and confirm offline capture works without venue wi-fi.
  • Test every QR on a phone on cell data, from the printed proof, before the final print order. The quiz and contact routes must be live on the final site.
Consent. Collect email only with an explicit opt-in, and get a separate opt-in before sending marketing texts (ask your attorney to confirm the wording and your obligations). The quiz is for adults 18 and older, and the event is adults only, so no minors-specific handling is built into the booth.
At the booth

Conversation script and follow-up.

Open with a question, not a pitch

  • "What part of your week runs you down the most?"
  • "When the pressure spikes, what do you notice first: your body, your head, or your temper?"
  • "Is this something you are dealing with yourself, or for a team you lead?"

Then, in this order

  1. Listen for one specific cost (sleep, decisions, a short fuse, energy by mid-afternoon).
  2. Name it back in one plain sentence, tied to the outcome: restored energy, focused clarity, joy under pressure.
  3. Offer the next step that fits: the quiz for an individual, the overview for an organization.
  4. Capture it right there: "Tap my phone and I will send it to you now."
  5. Give the card. If it is a real conversation, offer the keepable and book the call on the spot.

Stay inside the scope

Soma-Q is an education and training practice. In conversation: do not diagnose, do not promise results ("clients report" and "designed to" are the verbs), and do not describe the work as therapy or treatment. If someone describes a medical or mental health crisis, listen, say this is outside what you do, and point them to a licensed provider.

Your hesitation is reasonable and it is a choice, not a requirement. I found no study showing a founder's photo raises conversion at a booth, so this is my judgment.
